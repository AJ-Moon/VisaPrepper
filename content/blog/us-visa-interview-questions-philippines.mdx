Searching for **US visa interview questions in the Philippines** usually means an appointment is coming up and you want to know what the officer will ask. No one can give you the exact list. What you can prepare is the set of facts behind the questions: why you are going, who pays, what you will do afterward and, for students, why this school and this program.

This guide covers B1/B2 visitor visas first, then F-1 students. Every sample question and answer below is illustrative, written by our editorial team for practice, not an official question list.

## What the officer is deciding

For most nonimmigrant categories, U.S. law presumes that an applicant intends to immigrate until they show otherwise, and the burden of proof is on the applicant. The State Department's [Foreign Affairs Manual section on INA 214(b)](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM030201.html) explains this presumption. It also notes that a 214(b) refusal can mean another requirement of the category was not met, such as a student's funding.

In practice, a visitor interview keeps coming back to four topics:

| Topic | What the officer is trying to understand |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | What you will actually do in the U.S., and whether it fits a visitor visa |
| Length and plans | How long you will stay and what the trip looks like |
| Funding | Who pays for flights, accommodation and daily costs |
| Life afterward | The work, study, family or other commitments you return to |

The State Department's [visitor visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) says officers may ask for evidence of the purpose of your trip, your intent to depart and your ability to pay. For context, the State Department's [FY2025 adjusted refusal rates for B visas](https://travel.state.gov/content/dam/visas/Statistics/Non-Immigrant-Statistics/RefusalRates/FY25.pdf) list 30.76% for Philippine nationals in fiscal year 2025 (October 2024 to September 2025). That is a nationality-wide figure for B visas only, adjusted for refusals that were later overcome. It is not a prediction for any one case.

## Common US visa interview questions in the Philippines for B1/B2 visitors

These are the kinds of questions visitor applicants commonly prepare for. The officer may ask them in a different order, phrase them differently or skip most of them.

| Example question (illustrative) | What it is really about | Check before you practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is the purpose of your trip? | Whether the activity fits a visitor visa | The real reason, in one sentence |
| Who will you visit, and how are you related? | The relationship and the hosting arrangement | Names, relationship and where they live |
| How long will you stay? | Whether the length matches the purpose | Planned dates and why that length |
| Who is paying for the trip? | Whether funding is real and enough | Who pays for what, and from which funds |
| What do you do for work? | Your current situation in the Philippines | Employer, role, years employed, approved leave |
| Have you travelled abroad before? | Your travel history | Countries, years and whether you returned as planned |
| Do you have relatives in the U.S.? | Family context | Who and where, and whether your DS-160 lists them correctly |
| What will you do when you come back? | Commitments afterward | Your job, studies, family or business |

### Build each answer in three layers

A clear answer usually has three parts: a direct sentence, one specific detail and a fact you could support if asked.

**Example (illustrative), less clear:** “I will just visit and go around. My sister will take care of everything.”

**Example (illustrative), clearer:** “I'm visiting my sister in San Diego for two weeks in April for her son's graduation. I'm paying my airfare from my savings and staying at her home. My leave is approved, and I'm due back at work on May 2.”

The second answer works because each claim is specific and matches the DS-160, not because it is longer. Do not copy it: use your real facts, and check any detail you are unsure of.

## Local situations to prepare for

These situations are common among Filipino applicants. None is a problem in itself; each needs a clear, truthful explanation.

### Your employment certificate and your actual job

Many employed applicants bring a certificate of employment, recent payslips or a tax record such as BIR Form 2316 from their employer. The State Department's visitor page does not list these as required documents, but they help you state dates, position and salary accurately. Read your certificate before the interview and check that the job title, start date and salary match your DS-160. If you are self-employed or run a small business, practice describing what the business does and what you do in it, in plain words.

### Family members working abroad or living in the U.S.

Many applicants have a parent, spouse or sibling working overseas, or relatives in the United States. If someone abroad is paying part of your trip, know who, roughly how much and how the money reaches you. If U.S. relatives are hosting you, know their city and your exact relationship, and check their U.S. status rather than guess if the DS-160 asks. The visitor page says an invitation letter is not required to apply.

### Your travel history, or lack of it

Whether you have travelled widely or not at all, be ready to list past trips accurately, including the year and whether you returned as planned, and never overstate travel. The DS-160 asks about previous U.S. visits and refusals, so answer those questions completely.

### If you live and work outside the Philippines

Filipino workers based abroad should check where to apply. Current State Department [guidance on applying in your country of nationality or residence](https://travel.state.gov/en/newsroom/visas/2026/adjudicating-nonimmigrant-visa--niv--applicants-in-their-country.html) asks most nonimmigrant applicants to interview in their country of nationality or residence. If you apply where you live, you must be able to show that you reside there. If you apply outside both your country of nationality and your country of residence, the State Department warns that it may be harder to qualify, waits can be much longer, and fees are not refunded or transferred. Our [guide to preparing from any country](/blog/us-visa-interview-preparation-any-country) explains what changes by embassy.

### If you were refused before

A 214(b) refusal is not a permanent ban, and you may reapply with a new application and fee. What helps is being able to explain what has changed since then. Our [214(b) refusal guide](/blog/214b-visa-refusal-guide) explains how to prepare.

## F-1 student visa interview from the Philippines

Student interviews focus on whether you are a genuine student with a realistic plan and real funding. These are the kinds of questions students commonly prepare for:

| Example question (illustrative) | What it is really about | Check before you practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why did you choose this university? | Whether you chose the school and program for real reasons | How you compared schools, and how the program links to your past studies or work |
| Who is sponsoring your studies? | Whether the funding is real and committed | Each sponsor's name, relationship, job and the amount committed |
| How much will your first year cost? | Whether you know your own numbers | The tuition and living costs on your Form I-20, and how each is covered |
| What will you do after you graduate? | Your plans after the program | A realistic plan, in your own words |
| Why not study this program in the Philippines? | Whether your choice is considered | What you compared locally, stated without exaggeration |

Make a one-page funding note from your Form I-20 and your actual records. Label amounts in Philippine pesos (PHP) and U.S. dollars (USD), and keep yearly costs separate from monthly income. If a relative working abroad is a sponsor, know their job and what they have committed, and do not count hoped-for help as confirmed funding.

Two dated facts for students, as of 21 November 2026. ICE lists the [SEVIS I-901 fee](https://www.ice.gov/sevis/i901) as $350 for F-1 students, and its [I-901 fee FAQ](https://www.ice.gov/sevis/i901/faq) says to pay before your visa interview and allow at least three business days for the payment to be verified. Pay only on the official FMJfee.com site. The State Department has also [asked F, M and J applicants](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesperson/2025/06/announcement-of-expanded-screening-and-vetting-for-visa-applicants) to set their social media profiles to public for vetting, and the [U.S. Embassy in the Philippines](https://ph.usembassy.gov/announcement-of-expanded-screening-and-vetting-for-visa-applicants/) lists the other categories added since. List your accounts on the DS-160 accurately, and do not delete accounts to look different.

## Documents: official requirements versus supporting records

| Official items (State Department visitor and student pages, unless noted) | Supporting records you may choose to bring |
| --- | --- |
| Passport valid at least six months beyond your stay, unless an exemption applies | Certificate of employment, payslips or BIR Form 2316 |
| DS-160 confirmation page with barcode | Bank certificate or recent statements |
| Visa fee receipt, if you had to pay before the interview | Business registration, if self-employed |
| Printed photo, only if your DS-160 photo upload failed | PSA birth or marriage certificate, if a relationship matters to your trip |
| F-1: Form I-20 signed by you and the school; SEVIS I-901 fee paid before the interview (ICE) | F-1: transcripts, test scores and sponsor records |

The left column follows the State Department's [visitor](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) and [student](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/study/student-visa.html) visa pages, plus ICE for the SEVIS fee. In the Philippines, photos and fingerprints are taken at a separate Visa Application Center appointment (see below). The right column is optional: no official page lists it as required, and the officer may not ask to see any of it. None of the official pages we checked sets a minimum bank balance, and no document list guarantees approval. Your appointment instructions are the final checklist.

As of 21 November 2026, the State Department's fee schedule in [22 CFR 22.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-22/section-22.1) lists $185 for non-petition nonimmigrant visas (other than E visas), which include B1/B2 and F-1. The fee is generally not refunded if a visa is refused, and amounts can change, so confirm the current figure on official pages before paying.

## The U.S. Embassy in Manila: interviews, appointments and the day itself

For applicants in the Philippines, U.S. visa interviews are handled by the U.S. Embassy in Manila. Use the time and instructions on your own appointment confirmation. A few basics:

- **Two appointments.** The [U.S. Embassy in the Philippines visa page](https://ph.usembassy.gov/visas/) says visa applicants schedule two appointments: first at the Visa Application Center (VAC) for photo and fingerprint collection, then the interview at the Embassy. Check your confirmation for both and attend them in that order.
- **Most applicants now interview in person.** Since October 1, 2025, the State Department's [interview waiver update](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/News/visas-news/interview-waiver-update-sept-18-2025.html) generally limits waivers to diplomatic and official categories and a few renewal cases, mainly renewing, within 12 months of expiry, a full-validity B1/B2 visa issued when you were at least 18. You must also apply in your country of nationality or residence and have no previous refusal unless it was overcome or waived. An officer can still require an interview; first-time visitors and students should expect one.
- **Book only through official channels.** Start from the embassy's visa page and follow its links to the scheduling service. Apart from options shown on official pages, such as a temporary, optional [expedited B1/B2 appointment fee](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/09/2026-11513/schedule-of-fees-for-consular-services-department-of-state-and-overseas-embassies-and) offered at selected posts until December 31, 2026, be wary of anyone who promises an earlier slot or a result for a fee.
- **Interview language.** The State Department's [Manila instructions for immigrant visa interviews](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/Supplements/Supplements_by_Post/MNL-Manila.html) allow an interpreter for applicants who do not speak English or Filipino well enough. We could not confirm a separate statement for visitor and student interviews, so follow your appointment instructions.
- **What to wear and how long it takes.** We found no official dress code in the State Department and embassy sources we checked; clean, neat, comfortable clothes are a sensible choice. There is no fixed interview length: the window conversation is often brief, but security and queues make the visit much longer. Our interview day guide covers [what to wear](/blog/us-visa-interview-day-what-to-expect#what-to-wear-to-a-us-visa-interview) and [how long the interview takes](/blog/us-visa-interview-day-what-to-expect#how-long-does-the-interview-take).
- **Security and what to carry.** The embassy sets its own rules on phones, bags and other items. Read them on its website before the day, not in a group chat.
- **Travel restrictions.** The Philippines is not among the countries listed in [Presidential Proclamation 10998](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/19/2025-23570/restricting-and-limiting-the-entry-of-foreign-nationals-to-protect-the-security-of-the-united-states), which took effect on January 1, 2026. Lists can change, so check current guidance.

Family-based immigrant visa applicants follow separate Manila instructions; see our [family and immigrant visa preparation page](/visa-types/family-immigrant), or the [spouse visa interview guide](/blog/spouse-visa-interview-questions-ir1-cr1) for IR1/CR1 cases. For a walk-through of security, the window interview and what happens after, read our [interview day guide](/blog/us-visa-interview-day-what-to-expect).
