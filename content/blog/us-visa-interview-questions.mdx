Most US visa interview questions are short and plain: why are you going, who is paying, what will you do afterward. They are not trick questions. Each one asks you to explain part of your application that the consular officer needs to understand before deciding whether you qualify for the visa you applied for.

This hub covers the questions almost every applicant hears, then common questions by visa type, what each one checks and how to answer from your own facts. Every question below is an **illustrative example**, not an official list, and no list can predict what your officer will ask.

## What US visa interview questions are really checking

Two official rules explain most of the interview. First, the applicant carries the burden of showing they qualify. The State Department's Foreign Affairs Manual ([9 FAM 302.1](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM030201.html)) explains that most nonimmigrant applicants are presumed to be intending immigrants until they satisfy the officer otherwise; H-1B, L and V applicants are excluded from that presumption. A 214(b) refusal can also mean another requirement was not shown, such as an F-1 student's funding, not only weak "ties".

Second, the officer has your application. The State Department's [DS-160 page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/visa-information-resources/forms/ds-160-online-nonimmigrant-visa-application.html) says officers use the information you entered, combined with a personal interview, to decide eligibility. Immigrant visa cases use the DS-260 (K fiancé(e) visas use the DS-160).

So almost every question maps to one of six checks:

| Check | What the officer needs to understand | Where your facts live |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | What you will actually do in the U.S., and that it fits the visa | DS-160, itinerary, I-20, DS-2019 or petition |
| Plans and intent | What you will do afterward, where your category requires it | Your job, studies and family at home |
| Funding | Who pays, how much, and whether the money is available | Bank records, sponsor details, scholarship or employer letters |
| Qualifications | That you can do the course or job you describe | Degrees, transcripts, test scores, experience |
| Relationship | That a family relationship is genuine and correctly described | Marriage and birth records, shared history |
| Consistency | That spoken answers match what you submitted | Your submitted DS-160 or DS-260 and documents |

Not every check applies to every visa; a tourist, for example, has no U.S. course or job to show qualifications for.

## Questions almost every applicant hears

Whatever your visa type, expect some version of these. The wording is illustrative; your officer may phrase them differently, ask them in any order or skip them.

1. **"What is the purpose of your trip?"** Checks that what you will do fits your visa.
2. **"How long will you stay?"** Checks your dates against your form, program or petition.
3. **"Who is paying for this?"** Checks your funding and that the money is available.
4. **"What do you do now?"** Checks your current job or studies.
5. **"Why this school, employer or program?"** Checks that your choice is genuine and you qualify.
6. **"Have you travelled outside your country before?"** Checks your travel history against your form.
7. **"Have you ever been refused a U.S. visa?"** Checks your record and what has changed since.
8. **"Do you know anyone in the United States?"** Checks your U.S. contacts and their role in your plans.
9. **"What will you do afterward?"** Checks your plans, where your category requires it.

**Example (illustrative): "Why do you want to visit the United States?" (B1/B2)**

- Vague: "For tourism. I want to see America."
- Clearer: "For my niece's graduation in Chicago in May. I'll stay with my sister for two weeks, then fly home because I'm back at my teaching job on 2 June."

The clearer answer names the event, place, length of stay and reason for returning, and each part matches your form. Use your own facts.

## Common questions by visa type

Each table shows the check behind a few common questions and an example follow-up to practice. The full guides have longer lists, and each section ends with its practice page.

### B1/B2 visitor visa

| Question (illustrative) | What it checks | Possible follow-up (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why do you want to visit the United States? | Purpose fits a temporary visit | "Which cities, and for how long?" |
| Who will you stay with? | Accommodation and U.S. contacts | "What does your relative do there?" |
| Who is paying for the trip? | Funding | "How much do you expect to spend?" |
| What do you do at home? | Your current situation | "Who looks after that while you are away?" |

A homemaker, retiree or [parent visiting a child](/blog/b1-b2-visa-interview-questions-for-parents) answers the same checks from their own life: who manages things at home, who pays and why that length of stay. The State Department's [visitor visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) notes that officers may ask for evidence of the trip's purpose, your intent to depart and your ability to pay. Full guide: [B1/B2 interview questions](/blog/b1-b2-visa-interview-questions). Practice page: [B1/B2 practice](/visa-types/b1-b2).

### F1 student visa

| Question (illustrative) | What it checks | Possible follow-up (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why this university? | Purpose and a genuine choice | "Which other schools did you apply to?" |
| Why this course, and why now? | Academic fit | "How does it connect to your last degree or job?" |
| Who is funding your studies? | Funding | "What does your sponsor do?" or "What does the loan cover?" |
| What will you do after graduating? | Plans after study | "What kind of role, and where?" |

The State Department's [student visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/study/student-visa.html) says officers may ask for transcripts, test scores, evidence of intent to depart and proof of how you will pay. Know the yearly cost on your I-20 and how each funding source covers it ([funding answers practice](/blog/f1-visa-interview-who-will-pay)). ICE's [I-901 FAQ](https://www.ice.gov/sevis/i901/faq) says to pay the SEVIS fee before your visa interview and to allow at least three business days. As of 21 November 2026, the [I-901 fee page](https://www.ice.gov/sevis/i901) lists $350 for F-1 students (confirm there). Full guide: [F1 questions and answers](/blog/f1-visa-interview-questions-and-answers). Practice page: [F1 practice](/visa-types/f1).

### H1B work visa

| Question (illustrative) | What it checks | Possible follow-up (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What do you do day to day? | The job matches the petition | "Which tools or systems do you use?" |
| Who is your employer, and where will you work? | Employer and worksite | "Is that a client site or your employer's office?" |
| What is your educational background? | Qualifications | "How does your degree relate to this job?" |
| What is your salary? | Consistency with the petition | "Has anything changed since the petition was filed?" |

USCIS describes H-1B as a classification for [specialty occupations](https://www.uscis.gov/working-in-the-united-states/h-1b-specialty-occupations) that require a bachelor's or higher degree in a directly related specialty, or its equivalent, with the employer filing the petition. H-1B applicants are not subject to the INA 214(b) presumption of immigrant intent ([9 FAM 302.1-2(B)(5)](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM030201.html)). Officers still check that you qualify for the classification and are not otherwise ineligible, so expect detailed questions on whether the job you describe matches your petition. H-1B and H-4 applicants are also covered by the social media instruction described below. Full guide: [H1B interview questions](/blog/h1b-visa-interview-questions). Practice page: [H1B practice](/visa-types/h1b).

### H4 dependent visa

| Question (illustrative) | What it checks | Possible follow-up (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When and where did you get married? | Relationship | "How long have you known each other?" |
| What does your spouse do? | The H1B holder's job and status | "Which company, and in which city?" |
| What will you do in the United States? | Your plans | "What will a normal week look like?" |

You do not need every detail of your spouse's job, but you should be able to describe the employer, role and city in plain words. Your answers and your spouse's should agree because they describe the same facts. Full guide: [H4 interview questions](/blog/h4-visa-interview-questions). Practice page: [H4 practice](/visa-types/h4).

### J1 exchange visa

| Question (illustrative) | What it checks | Possible follow-up (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is your program, and who is your sponsor? | Purpose | "What will you do in a typical week?" |
| How is the program funded? | Funding | "What does the stipend cover?" |
| What will you do when it ends? | Plans afterward | "How will you use the experience at home?" |

The State Department's [exchange visitor page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/study/exchange.html) lists Form DS-2019, issued by your program sponsor, among the documents you need. An intern, a researcher and an au pair hear very different questions, so prepare for your own program category. Full guide: [J1 interview questions](/blog/j1-visa-interview-questions). Practice page: [J1 practice](/visa-types/j1).

### Family and immigrant visas

| Question (illustrative) | What it checks | Possible follow-up (illustrative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How and when did you meet? | The relationship is genuine | "When did you first meet in person?" |
| What does the petitioner do, and where do they live? | Knowledge of the petitioner | "Where will you live together?" |
| Who is your financial sponsor? | Support | "What is the sponsor's job?" |
| Have you been to the U.S. before? | History and consistency with your application | "Which visa did you have then?" |

These are consular interviews at a U.S. embassy or consulate, not USCIS interviews inside the United States. The State Department's [immigrant visa interview step](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/immigrate/the-immigrant-visa-process/step-10-prepare-for-the-interview/step-11-applicant-interview.html) tells applicants to bring the printed DS-260 confirmation page and original or certified civil documents. USCIS says most family-based immigrants use the [Form I-864 affidavit of support](https://www.uscis.gov/i-864) to show adequate means, so know who your sponsor is. Spouse cases: [IR1/CR1 interview questions](/blog/spouse-visa-interview-questions-ir1-cr1). Practice page: [family visa practice](/visa-types/family-immigrant).

### Children, parents and couples applying together

As of 21 November 2026, the State Department's [interview waiver update](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/News/visas-news/interview-waiver-update-sept-18-2025.html) (effective 1 October 2025) means most first-time nonimmigrant applicants need an in-person interview, and applicants under 14 and over 79 now generally do too, with limited exceptions; check your embassy's current instructions. For immigrant visas, follow your embassy's instructions for each family member. Check every family member's form, not just your own. Couples and parents travelling together should give matching answers about dates, hosts and funding because they describe the same facts, not because they rehearsed together.

## How to answer from your own facts

Good answers come from knowing your facts, not polished wording. Use this pattern for any question:

1. **Answer the question asked** in your first sentence.
2. **Anchor it** with one specific, checkable fact: a name, amount, date or place.
3. **Stop.** Let the officer decide what to ask next.
4. **Prepare the obvious follow-up.** For each answer, note the next question and the fact that answers it.

**Example (illustrative): an F1 funding question.**

- Vague: "My family will support me. We have enough money."
- Clearer: "My father is paying most of it. My I-20 lists about $52,000 a year. His savings cover the first year, and an education loan covers the rest."

The second answer is better not because it sounds confident, but because each part points to something real the officer can ask about. Use your own figures.

**Before you practice, write a one-page fact sheet:**

- Your purpose in one sentence
- Dates, length of stay or program dates
- Every funding source, with amount and currency
- School or employer name, city, and your course or role
- What you will return to, where your category requires it
- Previous U.S. visas, travel and any refusals
- Names and relationships of your U.S. contacts
- Anything that has changed since you submitted your form

Then check each line against your papers; our [documents checklist](/blog/us-visa-interview-documents-checklist) lists what to gather.

**What to avoid:**

- **Memorized paragraphs.** They break when the question is worded differently.
- **Answers copied from videos or forums.** They describe someone else's life.
- **Guessing numbers.** If you are unsure of a figure, check it before the interview.
- **Changing your story to match a wrong form.** Follow your embassy's correction instructions instead.
- **Hiding or deleting social media.** As of 21 November 2026, the State Department has asked F, M and J applicants ([June 2025](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesperson/2025/06/announcement-of-expanded-screening-and-vetting-for-visa-applicants)), H-1B and H-4 applicants ([from 15 December 2025](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/News/visas-news/announcement-of-expanded-screening-and-vetting-for-h-1b-and-dependent-h-4-visa-applicants.html)) and, under [later notices](https://travel.state.gov/en/newsroom/visas/2026/announcement-of-expanded-screening-and-vetting-for-visa-applicants.html), several other categories to set their social media profiles to public. Every applicant lists recent social media identifiers on the DS-160. List them accurately, and don't delete or hide accounts.

## Follow-ups, your form and previous refusals

Follow-ups often probe vague words in your first answer, such as "business", "family" or "enough money". Our [follow-up questions guide](/blog/visa-interview-follow-up-questions) shows how to practice them, and our guide for [nervous applicants](/blog/visa-interview-nervous-forget-answer) helps if you blank on an answer.

Before practicing, review your submitted DS-160, not just the confirmation page. You generally can't edit a DS-160 after you submit it. If something is wrong, follow your embassy's instructions; often that means completing a new DS-160 and updating your appointment with the new confirmation number. Our [DS-160 review guide](/blog/ds-160-interview-consistency) lists what to check.

If you were refused before, expect to be asked what has changed. A 214(b) refusal is not a permanent ineligibility ([9 FAM 302.1-2(B)(3)](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM030201.html)). The standard refusal letter in [9 FAM 403.10](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM040310.html) says you may reapply at any time with a new application and fee, and to be ready to show information you did not present before or how your circumstances have changed. Our [214(b) refusal guide](/blog/214b-visa-refusal-guide) explains how to prepare, and students can use our [F1 second-attempt guide](/blog/f1-visa-second-attempt-after-214b).

## Country guides

Before you prepare, check that you can apply. Check the current guidance before booking.

## A short practice routine

Start practicing at least a few days before your interview:

1. Write your fact sheet and compare it line by line with your submitted form.
2. Answer your visa type's questions aloud in random order and record yourself. Mark every answer that was vague, too long or not backed by a fact.
3. Have someone put follow-ups to your three weakest answers, or use an AI mock interview. The day before, re-read your form and documents; don't learn new lines.

For a day-by-day schedule, see the [7-day F1 practice plan](/blog/f1-visa-mock-interview-online-7-day-plan). For security, fingerprints and the window interview itself, see [what to expect on interview day](/blog/us-visa-interview-day-what-to-expect).

Practice in the language you are most comfortable with, then in the language you expect to use; our [Hindi and Urdu guide](/blog/us-visa-interview-in-hindi-urdu) covers practicing both ways. Check your embassy's appointment instructions for interview language; don't assume the interview will be in your preferred language.
