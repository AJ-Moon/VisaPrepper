B1/B2 interviews move fast. Officers typically have only a few minutes per applicant, and in that time they're trying to answer two questions: why are you going, and are you going to come back? Almost every question in a B1/B2 interview connects to one of those two.

This guide walks through what actually gets asked, how to prepare for both tourism and business travel, and how to avoid the most common reason B1/B2 applications get a second look.

## What a B1/B2 interview is checking

B1 covers business visitors (meetings, conferences, short trainings); B2 covers tourism, vacation, and visiting friends or family. Many applicants apply for both together. In both cases, the officer is assessing:

- Whether your stated purpose of travel is genuine and reasonably specific
- Whether you (or whoever is funding the trip) can afford it
- Whether you have strong enough ties to your home country to return after your visit

## How the interview typically flows

Most B1/B2 interviews open with a version of "what's the purpose of your trip?" and move quickly from there — often straight into finances and ties to home within the first minute or two. There's rarely time for a slow warm-up, so it helps to think of your opening answer as the one that needs to be sharpest, not something to ease into.

## Family visits from Pakistan, India and Bangladesh

If you are visiting a child, sibling, or other relative, prepare a short trip note: the reason, intended dates, accommodation, and who pays each cost. Do not copy another family's itinerary.

A retired parent can explain their real routine and responsibilities without inventing employment. Someone working in a family business can describe their own duties without claiming to own the business. If a relative pays for accommodation but not flights, make that distinction clear.

An invitation is not an approval guarantee. The [official visitor visa guidance](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) explains that an invitation letter is not required to apply.

For preparation closer to home, read the [Pakistan](/blog/us-visa-interview-questions-pakistan), [India](/blog/us-visa-interview-questions-india), or [Bangladesh](/blog/us-visa-interview-questions-bangladesh) guide. These examples are practice exercises, not a prediction of interview questions.

## Your trip, costs and plans afterward

### Purpose of your trip

Practice questions:

- Why are you traveling to the United States?
- How long do you plan to stay?
- Where will you be staying, and with whom?
- What will you do during your trip?

Specific, simple answers work better than long explanations. "I'm visiting my sister in Chicago for two weeks for her wedding" is stronger than a vague, drawn-out description.

### Finances

Practice questions:

- Who is paying for this trip?
- What is your occupation, and what is your income?
- Do you have savings to support the trip?
- If someone in the U.S. is hosting you, how do you know them, and do you know their situation?

Even when someone else is fully funding your trip, it's worth being able to describe your own financial situation at home too — officers sometimes ask both, since a sponsor's willingness to pay doesn't by itself establish your reasons to return.

If a friend or relative in the U.S. is covering your costs, be ready to explain who they are and roughly how you know them — not just "he's paying for everything."

### Ties to home

This is the section most B1/B2 refusals under [Section 214(b)](/blog/214b-visa-refusal-guide) come back to. Officers are looking for evidence that you have reasons to return home: a job, a business, family responsibilities, property, or ongoing studies.

Practice questions:

- What is your job, and how long have you worked there?
- Who is in your immediate family, and are they traveling with you?
- Do you own property or run a business at home?
- When are you expected back at work or school?

Vague or thin answers here — no job, no clear plans, no timeline to return — are one of the most common reasons a B1/B2 application doesn't move forward.

Ties to home aren't limited to owning property or being married — a stable job with a return date, enrollment in an ongoing degree program, or clear family responsibilities can all count. If you're young and haven't accumulated property or a long work history yet, focus on whatever genuine, specific commitments you do have, rather than assuming you have nothing to offer here.

### Travel history

Practice questions:

- Have you traveled internationally before?
- Have you visited the United States before?
- Did you return home on time after previous trips?

A clean travel history with on-time returns is a point in your favor. If this is your first international trip, that's not disqualifying — just be ready to explain your situation honestly.

### Business travel specifics

If you're traveling on B1 for business, expect more targeted questions:

- What does your company do, and what is your role?
- Who will you be meeting, and what is the purpose of the meetings?
- Is your company paying for the trip, or the host company?
- Why does this meeting need to happen in person, in the United States?

If your U.S. partner or client has sent an invitation letter, know what it says and be ready to describe the relationship in your own words — reciting the letter back isn't the same as being able to explain it naturally.

### A realistic example

A weak version of a family-visit answer: "I want to visit my family and see America." A stronger version, describing the same real trip: "I'm visiting my parents in New Jersey for three weeks over winter break — I work as a pharmacist back home and I'll return right after the new year." Both applicants may have identical, genuine intentions; the second answer simply gives the officer something concrete to work with in the little time available.

## Sample B1/B2 interview questions to practice with

1. What is the purpose of your visit to the United States?
2. How long do you plan to stay, and where?
3. Who is paying for this trip?
4. What do you do for work, and how long have you worked there?
5. Do you have family or property in your home country?
6. Have you been to the United States before?
7. Have you ever had a visa refused?

## Mistakes that weaken B1/B2 answers

- **Being vague about the itinerary.** "I want to see America" is weaker than a specific, believable plan.
- **Inconsistency with the DS-160.** Your interview answers about dates, funding, and purpose should match what you entered on your DS-160. See our guide on [DS-160 consistency](/blog/ds-160-interview-consistency).
- **Thin ties to home.** Not mentioning your job, family, or responsibilities at home when asked leaves the officer with less reason to believe you'll return.
- **Overexplaining.** Given how short these interviews are, long, unfocused answers can come across as uncertain rather than thorough.
