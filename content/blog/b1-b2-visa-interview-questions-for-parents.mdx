Parents visiting an adult son or daughter in the United States, whether for a graduation, a new grandchild or a long-awaited family visit, usually face the same core questions: why you are going, how long you will stay, who pays and what your life at home looks like. One point is easy to miss. The officer is assessing **you**, not your child.

This guide to B1/B2 visa interview questions for parents groups the questions parents often practice, shows how to build each answer from your own facts and summarizes official rules for older applicants, checked on 21 November 2026. Every example question and answer below is illustrative, not an official list or an embassy script.

## What the officer is weighing when a parent applies

Visiting family is a recognized visitor purpose. The federal rule defining B-2 "pleasure" travel lists [visits with friends or relatives](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-22/section-41.31) alongside tourism and rest. The purpose is rarely the hard part.

Under INA 214(b), every B1/B2 applicant is presumed to intend to immigrate until they show otherwise, and the State Department's manual places the [burden of proof on the applicant](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM030201.html) (9 FAM 302.1-2). For a parent, that means explaining a genuine, temporary visit and the life you are returning to. Your child's status is context, not a sponsorship requirement, and your child cannot qualify on your behalf.

## B1/B2 visa interview questions for parents, by topic

| Topic | Example questions (illustrative) | What a clear answer covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Why are you traveling? Why now? | The occasion in one sentence, with a date if there is one |
| Your child | What does your son do? Where does your daughter live? | City, studies or work in plain words, and how long they have been there |
| Length of stay | How long will you stay? Why that long? | Planned dates and the reason for that length |
| Who pays | Who is paying for this trip? | Which costs you cover and which your child covers |
| Activities in the U.S. | What will you do there? Will you work? | Family time, helping at home or sightseeing, and that you are not taking a job |
| Life at home | What do you do? Who lives with you? | Work, pension, home, family duties: only what is true |
| Travel history | Have you traveled abroad before? | Countries, rough dates and your return as planned |
| Previous U.S. visits | When were you last in the U.S.? How long did you stay? | Dates of earlier stays, when you left, and why this trip's length differs, if it does |
| Your application | Has anyone filed an immigrant petition for you? | A truthful answer that matches your DS-160 |

A visitor visa does not allow employment: the State Department's [visitor visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) lists work among the activities that need a different visa. If your last B1/B2 visa expired less than 12 months ago, read the renewal waiver section below.

For the general visitor reference, see our [B1/B2 interview question guide](/blog/b1-b2-visa-interview-questions), or the [U.S. visa interview questions hub](/blog/us-visa-interview-questions) for every visa type. This page covers what changes when the visitor is a parent.

### Questions about your child's life in the U.S.

Expect questions about your child. You do not need to know their immigration paperwork, only the basics of their life.

| Your child is... | Know before the interview | Be careful about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A student | University, course and graduation date | Saying a student will fund the whole trip if they realistically cannot |
| Working on a work visa | Employer, city and their role in everyday words | Guessing job titles or salaries you have not checked |
| A green card holder or U.S. citizen | Where they live and what they do | Leaving out an immigrant petition filed for you |

If your child has filed an immigrant petition for you, that is a separate process from a visit. Our [family and immigrant visa page](/visa-types/family-immigrant) covers that route. Consider qualified advice on how the two fit together.

## Build each answer in three parts

Instead of a long speech, try this structure:

1. **The fact.** One sentence that answers the question asked.
2. **One supporting detail.** A date, a place, a name or an amount.
3. **The link home, when it fits.** What you are returning to, in your own words.

Then stop and let the officer ask the next question. Our guide to [visa interview follow-up questions](/blog/visa-interview-follow-up-questions) shows how to practice that next step.

**Example (illustrative), India.** Meera, 62, is a retired bank employee in Pune. Her son is finishing a master's degree in Arizona.

- Vague: "My son is there, so we want to go and see America."
- Clearer: "My son graduates from his master's program in May. I plan to attend and stay about four weeks. My mother, who is 85, lives with me. My daughter will look after her while I am away, and I am back for her medical review in June."

**Example (illustrative), Pakistan.** Tariq and Nasreen live in Lahore. Their daughter in Houston is expecting her first child.

- Vague: "Our daughter needs us."
- Clearer, from Nasreen: "Our daughter's baby is due in March. I plan to stay about three months to help after the birth. My husband will come for six weeks, because he runs a pharmacy with a partner and needs to be back."

Each parent explains their own plan, and either may be asked. These people are invented; use only your own details. Country detail: our [India guide](/blog/us-visa-interview-questions-india) and [India practice page](/visa-interview-practice/india), or our [Pakistan guide](/blog/us-visa-interview-questions-pakistan) and [Pakistan practice page](/visa-interview-practice/pakistan).

## Three common visits and the follow-ups they raise

### Attending a graduation

The fixed date makes the purpose easy to state. Rehearse follow-ups about your other plans and why the stay is that length. If you plan sightseeing after the ceremony, say so plainly.

### Helping after a grandchild is born

These visits tend to be longer because due dates move and help is needed for weeks. Be ready to explain why you chose that length and how your responsibilities at home are covered while you are away.

Be open about the birth. The visitor regulation excludes travel whose [primary purpose is giving birth to obtain U.S. citizenship for a child](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-22/section-41.31), and a [2026 executive order](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/11/2026-16404/ending-birth-tourism) also targets efforts to help another person enter on a visa for that purpose. Helping a daughter who already lives in the U.S. after her baby arrives is a different purpose, so describe it plainly and truthfully: where she lives, her status in everyday words, the due date and how long you will help. Expect follow-up questions.

The visa itself does not set your length of stay. A border officer decides it when you arrive, within the limits of [8 CFR 214.2(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/section-214.2) (see the FAQ below), and records it on your I-94. Plan the stay you actually need and be ready to explain it.

### A general family visit

Without a fixed event, "why now?" matters more. A real reason is enough, such as your retirement or years since you last saw your child. Do not stretch the plan to fill the longest stay you think might be allowed.

## Who pays: separate each cost

A child may pay for flights, or parents may stay in the child's home. You can say so, as long as you can explain the split.

| Cost | Who pays (example) | How you might say it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flights | Your child | "My son has offered to pay for our flights." |
| Accommodation | Staying with your child | "We will stay in his apartment in Phoenix." |
| Daily expenses and insurance | You | "I will cover my own expenses from my pension." |

Even when your child pays most costs, expect questions about your own income, pension or business. Keep amounts in your own currency (INR, PKR or another) clearly separate from U.S. dollar figures, and never overstate savings. Check that the funding answer on your DS-160 matches, especially if your child filled in the form for you. Our [DS-160 consistency guide](/blog/ds-160-interview-consistency) explains what to review.

## Ties at home: describe your real life

No checklist of ties guarantees a visa; officers look at your overall circumstances. For parents, relevant facts often include:

- A pension, job or business you return to
- A home you own or rent and look after
- Other children, grandchildren or an elderly parent who depend on you
- Health appointments, community roles or religious commitments with dates
- Earlier trips abroad and your on-time returns

Retired parents sometimes worry they have "no ties" because they no longer work. Describe your real routine instead of inventing a job. If you were refused before, say so truthfully. Our [214(b) refusal guide](/blog/214b-visa-refusal-guide) explains what that refusal means and how to prepare a new application.

## Invitation letters: what they can and cannot do

The State Department's [visitor visa guidance](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) says a letter of invitation or Affidavit of Support is not needed to apply and, if you bring one, is not one of the factors used to decide the visa. A short note from your child can still help you learn their address, the event date and who pays for what, so you can explain the facts yourself.

## Older parents: interview waivers and appointment support

Rules for older applicants changed in 2025. The current rules come from the State Department's [interview waiver update of 18 September 2025](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/News/visas-news/interview-waiver-update-sept-18-2025.html), effective 1 October 2025, which replaced a July 2025 notice that first ended the age-based waiver:

- Applicants over 79 no longer qualify for an age-based waiver and generally need an in-person interview.
- The main remaining waiver for visitors covers renewing a full-validity B1/B2 visa within 12 months of its expiry, if you were at least 18 when it was issued.
- You must also apply in your country of nationality or residence (the [personal appearance](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-22/section-41.102) regulation sets the same limit), have no previous refusal (unless it was overcome or waived) and have no apparent ineligibility.
- A consular officer can still require anyone to interview.

Check your embassy's current instructions before assuming you qualify.

If health or mobility is a concern, ask early. The U.S. missions in [India](https://in.usembassy.gov/visas/important-visa-information/) and [Pakistan](https://pk.usembassy.gov/visas/important-visa-information/) ask applicants to request reasonable accommodation as soon as they schedule. Both pages say the consular section waiting room is wheelchair accessible, and that an interview waiver on medical grounds is granted only in extreme situations, such as a medical condition requiring an air ambulance. This is separate from the renewal waiver above. Confirm arrangements with the specific post where you will interview. For the day itself, read [what to expect at your U.S. visa interview](/blog/us-visa-interview-day-what-to-expect).

## Before the interview: a parent's checklist

**What the State Department lists for a visitor interview:**

- A passport valid at least six months beyond your planned stay, unless your country is exempt
- Your own DS-160 confirmation page (each parent submits a separate application)
- The fee receipt, if your post requires payment first. As of 21 November 2026, the [State Department fee schedule](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-22/section-22.1) lists $185 for a B1/B2 application. Confirm the current amount before paying. The fee is generally [not refunded if a visa is refused](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM060105.html), and depending on your nationality a separate reciprocity fee may apply if the visa is approved

**Preparation notes and optional papers** (an officer may or may not ask to see them):

- A one-page trip note: purpose, dates, where you will stay and who pays each cost
- Your child's address, phone number, and university or employer name
- A copy of your child's status document, such as an I-20, approval notice or green card, and any graduation or event notice
- Your own pension, income or bank records, and approximate dates of earlier trips abroad and U.S. stays

**Rules to check for your nationality:**

- If you live outside your country of nationality, where you should apply under the State Department's [current application-location guidance](https://travel.state.gov/en/newsroom/visas/2026/adjudicating-nonimmigrant-visa--niv--applicants-in-their-country.html)
- Whether your nationality is subject to U.S. entry and visa restrictions under [Presidential Proclamation 10998](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/12/19/2025-23570/restricting-and-limiting-the-entry-of-foreign-nationals-to-protect-the-security-of-the-united-states) (in effect since 1 January 2026; it suspends B1/B2 visas for nationals of many listed countries). Check the State Department's current guidance before booking
- Whether your nationality appears on the [visa bond country list](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/News/visas-news/countries-subject-to-visa-bonds.html). A [2026 rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/03/2026-15726/visas-visa-bond-program) lets officers require a bond of up to $20,000 from B1/B2 applicants of listed nationalities. If a bond is required, the visa holder may enter and leave the U.S. only through commercial airports, not land or sea crossings, so check the current conditions
