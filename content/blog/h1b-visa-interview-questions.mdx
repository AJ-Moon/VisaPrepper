H1B interviews are less about your personal story and more about your job: your employer, your role, and whether your qualifications genuinely match a specialty occupation. That makes them different from F1 or B1/B2 interviews, and it means the most common weak point is applicants who understand their work but struggle to explain it clearly out loud, under time pressure, to someone unfamiliar with their specific field.

## What an H1B interview is actually checking

Officers reviewing an H1B application are generally trying to confirm that your role qualifies as a specialty occupation, that your qualifications genuinely match that role, and that your answers are consistent with the petition your employer filed on your behalf (Form I-129 and the underlying Labor Condition Application). Unlike F1 or B1/B2, home-country ties are typically a smaller part of an H1B interview — the focus is much more heavily on the job itself.

## How the interview typically flows

Most H1B interviews open by asking you to describe your role in your own words. This is a good moment to give a clear, one- or two-sentence summary — your title, your main responsibility, and who you work for — before the officer drills into specifics. Applicants who launch into a long, unfocused description of their whole career history here often struggle more with the sharper follow-up questions that come next, simply because they've used up the interview's early momentum without giving a clear anchor point.

## Common H1B interview topics

### Your role and day-to-day duties

Practice questions:

- What is your job title, and what do you do day to day?
- Can you walk me through a typical week in your role?
- Who do you report to, and who reports to you, if anyone?
- What tools, systems, or methods do you use regularly in your work?

The goal is a clear, specific description that matches the duties listed in your petition — not a title alone. If you can describe a recent, concrete task you completed, that often lands better than an abstract description of your responsibilities.

### Your qualifications

Practice questions:

- What is your educational background, and how does it relate to this role?
- Do you have a specialized degree or equivalent experience required for this position?
- Why does this role require someone with your specific background?

If your degree field doesn't obviously match your job title, be ready to explain the connection clearly — this is a common follow-up point, and vague answers here are one of the more frequent weak spots in H1B interviews.

### Your employer and worksite

Practice questions:

- What does your employer do?
- Where will you be working, and is that different from your employer's main office?
- How long has the company been in business, and roughly how large is it?

### Third-party placements and IT consulting structures

If you work for a staffing, consulting, or IT services company and will be placed at a client's site rather than your employer's own office, expect additional questions:

- What is the relationship between your employer and the end client?
- Who directly supervises your day-to-day work — your employer or someone at the client site?
- Why does the end client need this specific role filled?

Answer these plainly rather than describing only your official employer, since the officer is specifically trying to understand the real reporting and work structure, not just the name on your paycheck. If your placement has changed since your petition was filed — a new client, a new project, or a new site — that's worth discussing with your employer's immigration counsel before your interview, since it can affect whether an amended petition is needed.

### Salary and the Labor Condition Application

Your employer's Labor Condition Application (LCA) commits them to paying at least the prevailing wage for your role and location. You're not expected to know prevailing wage calculations, but you should be able to state your actual salary confidently and know that it matches what's on file — a mismatch here, even an honest one caused by a recent raise or a payroll change, is worth clarifying with your employer before your appointment rather than during it.

### Petition consistency

Practice questions:

- What is your annual salary?
- When does your employment start, or when did it start?
- Has anything about your role changed since your petition was filed?

Your answers here should match what's in your I-129 petition and LCA. See our [U.S. visa interview documents checklist](/blog/us-visa-interview-documents-checklist) for the H1B-specific documents worth reviewing beforehand.

### Employment history and renewals

Practice questions:

- What did you do before this role?
- If you changed employers recently, why did you make that move?
- Have you held H1B status before, and with which employer?
- If this is an extension or renewal, has your role changed since your last approval?

Renewal and extension interviews are often shorter, but don't assume they're automatic — you should still be ready to describe your current role and employer confidently, not just refer back to a previous approval.

## Sample H1B interview questions to practice with

1. What is your job title and what are your main responsibilities?
2. How does your educational background qualify you for this role?
3. What does your employer's company do?
4. Where will you be working, and who supervises you there?
5. What is your salary, and when does your employment begin?
6. Why did this employer choose to sponsor you specifically?
7. Have you held H1B status before?
8. What's the relationship between your employer and the client site, if applicable?

Run through these questions out loud, then have someone unfamiliar with your field listen and tell you honestly whether your answer actually made sense to them — a good test for whether you're explaining your role clearly or relying on jargon a general audience, including a consular officer, may not know.

## Mistakes that weaken H1B interview answers

- **Reciting a job title without explaining the actual work.** "I'm a software engineer" isn't enough on its own — be ready to describe what that means day to day, with a concrete example if possible.
- **A weak link between degree and duties.** If your qualifications and your role seem disconnected, address that directly instead of hoping it doesn't come up.
- **Not knowing basic facts about your employer.** You don't need financial details, but you should know what the company does and your place in it.
- **Describing only your official employer in a third-party placement.** If you work at a client site, explain that structure clearly rather than letting the officer piece it together through follow-up questions.
- **Inconsistency with the petition.** Salary, job title, or worksite that doesn't match your I-129 is worth resolving with your employer before your interview, not during it.
