Most of the **US visa interview process** happens away from the interview window. On the day you queue, pass security, hand over documents, give fingerprints and wait; the conversation with the consular officer is usually the shortest part. Knowing the order of events keeps your attention on what matters: explaining your real plans clearly.

This guide covers interview day for nonimmigrant applicants such as B1/B2 visitors, F-1 students, J-1 exchange visitors and H-1B or H-4 applicants. Immigrant visa interviews (for example, spouse visas) follow different instructions; see our [spouse visa interview guide](/blog/spouse-visa-interview-questions-ir1-cr1). Each embassy and consulate (each "post") sets its own logistics, so treat this page as a map and your appointment instructions as the rulebook.

## First, confirm you still need an in-person interview

Since October 1, 2025, most nonimmigrant visa applicants generally need an in-person interview, including children under 14 and adults over 79. The State Department's [September 18, 2025 interview waiver update](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/News/visas-news/interview-waiver-update-sept-18-2025.html) limits waivers mainly to diplomatic and official categories and to renewals of a full-validity B1/B2 visa or Border Crossing Card within 12 months of expiry, where the applicant was at least 18 when it was issued. Other conditions apply, such as applying in your country of nationality or usual residence and having no previous refusal unless it was overcome or waived. Officers can still require anyone to interview. The underlying regulation, [22 CFR 41.102](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-22/section-41.102), requires applicants aged 14 to 79 to appear in person unless waived; the 2025 policy now generally requires younger and older applicants to interview as well.

Unsure which post should handle your case? Our [embassy preparation guide](/blog/us-visa-interview-preparation-any-country) explains the State Department's nationality-or-residence guidance.

## The US visa interview process, step by step

### Where interview day fits in the process

The order varies by post, as the State Department's [visitor visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) notes, but most nonimmigrant applicants follow this sequence:

1. Complete the online DS-160 and keep the confirmation page. Our [DS-160 review guide](/blog/ds-160-interview-consistency) covers what to check.
2. Pay the visa application fee, if your post requires payment before the interview.
3. Book the interview through the scheduling service linked from your post's official website, generally in the country where you live.
4. Give fingerprints, usually at the interview, though some locations take them at a separate appointment.
5. Attend the interview, then get your passport back or follow up on a 221(g) or refusal.

### Interview day, stage by stage

| Stage | What usually happens | What to check in your post's instructions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Before you leave | Gather your passport, confirmation pages and category documents | The exact document list and fee receipt rules |
| 2. Arrival and security | Show your appointment and pass screening | Arrival time, prohibited items, who may accompany you |
| 3. Intake and fingerprints | Staff check documents and scan fingerprints; you wait | Whether biometrics happen today or at a separate appointment |
| 4. Window interview | An officer reviews your application and asks questions | Interview language arrangements |
| 5. Decision | The officer generally tells you the outcome or next step | What any letter you receive asks you to do |
| 6. Afterward | Passport return, a 221(g) follow-up, or planning after a refusal | Passport delivery or pickup method |

## Before you leave home

### Documents to carry

The State Department's [visitor visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/tourism-visit/visitor.html) and [student visa page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/study/student-visa.html) list the core items. Your post's instructions are the final checklist.

- **Passport** valid for at least six months beyond your intended period of stay, unless an agreement with your country exempts you.
- **DS-160 confirmation page** with its barcode. The [Foreign Affairs Manual](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM040303.html) describes applicants hand-carrying this page to the consular section.
- **Appointment confirmation and fee receipt**, if your post asks for them.
- **Category documents**, such as Form I-20 for F-1 students (with the [SEVIS I-901 fee](https://www.ice.gov/sevis/i901) paid before the interview) or Form DS-2019 for J-1 applicants, plus records about your purpose, finances and plans.

Our [US visa interview documents checklist](/blog/us-visa-interview-documents-checklist) covers each category in detail, including work, dependent and immigrant cases.

### The night before

1. Reread your submitted DS-160 answers if you kept a copy; the officer works from that form. Our [DS-160 review guide](/blog/ds-160-interview-consistency) lists what to check.
2. Say your purpose, dates, funding and plans after the trip out loud, once, in plain sentences.
3. Check the route, the exact building and the security rules, and arrange somewhere to leave anything you can't take in.

## Arriving and getting through security

Security procedures differ by post, so read the security section of your own post's website, reachable through the [official U.S. embassy directory](https://www.usembassy.gov/). The [U.S. Embassy and Consulate in Vietnam's nonimmigrant visa FAQ](https://vn.usembassy.gov/frequently-asked-questions-nonimmigrant-visas/) says that, as is standard practice worldwide, no third parties may attend a nonimmigrant visa interview, with limited exceptions such as a parent or legal guardian accompanying an applicant under 17.

**One official example.** When we reviewed it in 2026, the [U.S. Embassy and Consulates in India's visa information page](https://in.usembassy.gov/visas/important-visa-information/) listed mobile phones and other electronic devices, large bags and backpacks, food and drink, sealed envelopes and spray perfume among items not permitted inside. Small hand-carried bags or zip folders holding application papers were allowed, and the page said there was no facility to store prohibited items. Other posts may have different rules.

Practical points for any post:

- Arrive at the time your instructions give. The [U.S. Embassy and Consulates in Brazil's nonimmigrant visa FAQ](https://br.usembassy.gov/frequently-asked-questions-on-non-immigrant-visas/), for example, asks applicants to arrive as close as possible to their appointment time.
- Carry papers in a clear folder, in the order you are likely to need them.
- If phones are not allowed, plan how you will get home or reach the people waiting for you.
- Raise any accessibility needs with the post in advance.

## Document intake and fingerprints

Inside, staff typically check your passport, confirmation pages and appointment, then scan your fingerprints, unless your post took them at an earlier appointment. If a medical condition prevents fingerprinting, ask the post about options; the India page mentions fingerprint waivers for certain fingers.

Then you wait to be called. Keep your documents in order; don't cram new answers.

## The window interview

At the window, the officer usually has your application on screen, checks your passport and asks about your purpose, plans, finances and circumstances. They may ask for a document, or for none at all. Posts set their own interview-language arrangements, so check yours.

### How long does the interview take?

There is no fixed official duration; it varies by case and by post. The [U.S. Embassy and Consulate in Vietnam's nonimmigrant visa FAQ](https://vn.usembassy.gov/frequently-asked-questions-nonimmigrant-visas/) says an officer may interview 100 or more applicants on a typical day, which allows only a few minutes for each. It adds that a thoroughly completed application form contains most of the information needed, and extra documents are examined only when the officer needs clarification.

## How to answer at the window

Interviews are often short (one post's FAQ cites only a few minutes per applicant), so the officer can't hear your life story. A simple framework helps: **answer, anchor, stop**.

1. **Answer** the exact question in your first sentence.
2. **Anchor** it with one concrete, true fact that matches your application: a date, an amount, a course or an employer.
3. **Stop** and let the officer lead. A follow-up question is normal, not a bad sign.

| Example (illustrative) | Answer that drifts | Answer that anchors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visitor: "What is the purpose of your trip?" | "I have always wanted to see America, and my family says it is very beautiful..." | "I'm attending my daughter's graduation in Boston in May and staying with her for three weeks." |
| Student: "Who is paying for your studies?" | "My family will manage everything." | "My father is paying tuition from his savings, and my living costs are covered by an education loan that has already been approved." |

These examples show structure only. Never borrow a fact: an anchor helps only when it is true and matches your DS-160 and documents.

Habits that help at the window:

- If you didn't hear or understand a question, ask the officer to repeat it. If you don't know something, say so plainly instead of guessing.
- Hand over a document when the officer asks for it, or when it directly answers the question.
- Stay polite whatever the outcome. Arguing does not change a decision.

## What to wear to a US visa interview

We found no official dress code in the State Department and embassy sources we checked, and clothing is not a visa requirement. A few practical choices make the day easier:

- Wear clean, neat clothes you would choose for a bank or office appointment. You don't need a suit, and no official rule we found bans jeans.
- Choose shoes you can stand in for a while, and dress for the weather in case part of the wait is outdoors.
- Keep accessories simple so screening is quick, and skip anything your post prohibits. India's list, for example, includes spray perfume.

## What happens after the interview

| Outcome | What it means | What to do next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visa approved | You were found eligible; the passport is usually kept so the visa can be printed | Follow the delivery or pickup instructions, then check the name, visa type and dates |
| 221(g) refusal | More documents, information or administrative processing are needed before a decision | Do what your instructions ask; timelines vary by case |
| 214(b) refusal | The officer was not satisfied, at that interview, that you met the category's requirements | Read the refusal letter; you may reapply with a new application |

### If your visa is approved

Posts return passports in different ways, such as courier delivery or collection from a pickup location, and timing varies by post. The [Vietnam FAQ](https://vn.usembassy.gov/frequently-asked-questions-nonimmigrant-visas/), for example, estimates courier delivery at 1-2 business days for metropolitan addresses and 3-5 business days for rural ones once a visa is issued. A 221(g) or administrative processing has no fixed timeline. Avoid firm travel plans and ticket purchases until you have the visa and have checked its details. Depending on nationality, a separate issuance (reciprocity) fee may be charged after approval, as the [Foreign Affairs Manual](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM040304.html) explains.

### If you receive a 221(g)

Under [9 FAM 302.1-8](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM030201.html), a 221(g) refusal applies when an application doesn't meet legal requirements, for example when required information or documents are missing. It is legally a refusal even if it is later overcome. The State Department's [visa denials page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/visa-information-resources/visa-denials.html) and [9 FAM 403.10](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM040310.html) add that a 221(g) refusal can also mean the case needs administrative processing. The officer tells you at the end of the interview if [administrative processing](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/visa-information-resources/administrative-processing-information.html) is needed. How long it takes varies by case, and the result can be issuance or continued ineligibility.

If you resolve the request within one year of the refusal, 9 FAM 403.10 says you generally don't need a new DS-160 or a new application fee. No agent or website can speed up or predict the outcome.

### If you are refused under 214(b)

A 214(b) refusal is not a permanent ban, and there is no appeal. The standard refusal letter in [9 FAM 403.10](https://fam.state.gov/FAM/09FAM/09FAM040310.html) says you may reapply at any time with a new application, a new fee and a new interview. The letter adds that if you reapply, you should be ready to give information you didn't present before or show that your circumstances have changed. The [State Department's visa denials page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/visa-information-resources/visa-denials.html) explains both refusal types.

Our [214(b) refusal guide](/blog/214b-visa-refusal-guide) explains how to review what happened. Students can also read the [F-1 second attempt guide](/blog/f1-visa-second-attempt-after-214b).
