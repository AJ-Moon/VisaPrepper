J1 covers a wider range of programs than almost any other U.S. visa category — university students on exchange, visiting research scholars, interns, trainees, au pairs, camp counselors, and summer work-and-travel participants all apply under the same visa type. That range is exactly why generic "J1 interview questions" lists tend to feel unhelpful: an au pair's interview and a visiting scholar's interview cover genuinely different ground, even though both are J1.

This guide breaks down what a J1 interview actually focuses on, how that differs by category, and how to prepare around your specific program rather than a one-size-fits-all script.

## What a J1 interview is actually checking

Across every category, officers are generally trying to confirm four things:

- **Your program is genuine** — you were actually selected through a real exchange program with a designated sponsor
- **You understand your own role** — what you'll be doing, where, and for how long
- **Your funding is credible** — however your program is paid for, the numbers should make sense
- **You have sufficient ties to your home country** consistent with the exchange visitor category, since J1 is a nonimmigrant visa

Because J1 sponsors range from universities to au pair placement agencies to summer work-travel organizations, officers are also somewhat familiar with common patterns for each category — which means a vague or generic answer stands out more than it might in a less-scrutinized category.

## How the interview typically unfolds

Most J1 interviews open with a version of "what is your program about?" — a simple, direct opportunity to explain your category, your host organization, and your role in one or two clear sentences. From there, questions usually branch into funding, your plans during the program, and your ties to home. Applicants who stumble here often do so not because their program is unusual, but because they haven't practiced summarizing it concisely out loud.

## Common J1 interview topics by category

### Program purpose and sponsor (all categories)

Practice questions:

- What is your program, and who is the sponsor organization?
- Why were you selected for this program?
- What will you actually be doing day to day?
- How long does your program run, and what's your program end date?

Know your program category and your sponsor's name clearly — this is almost always the first question, and hesitating on it can set an uneasy tone for the rest of the interview.

### Students and scholars

If you're a university student on exchange, a visiting researcher, or a short-term scholar, expect follow-up on:

- How this exchange connects to your degree, research, or academic career at home
- Which host institution or lab you'll be working with, and who your host supervisor is
- What you'll bring back to your home institution or employer afterward

### Interns and trainees

Internship and trainee categories (distinct from H1B, and generally shorter-term and structured around a formal training plan) often draw questions like:

- What does your training plan actually involve?
- How does this align with your field of study or current career?
- Who is your host company, and what industry are they in?

Be ready to describe your training plan as a learning experience tied to your home career, not as ordinary employment — that distinction matters for how these categories are structured.

### Au pairs and camp counselors

These categories are less about career alignment and more about the specific placement:

- Which family or camp will you be working with, and where are they located?
- What are your responsibilities day to day?
- Do you have relevant experience — childcare, coaching, camp work — from home?

### Work-and-travel participants

Officers may ask more pointedly about the seasonal, temporary nature of the placement:

- What is your specific job and work location for the season?
- What will you do once the work-and-travel period ends?
- Why are you applying through this particular sponsor program?

## Funding

Practice questions:

- How is your program funded — stipend, personal savings, sponsor support, host family provisions?
- If there's a gap between your program costs (travel, housing, insurance) and your funding, how will you cover it?
- For unpaid or lower-stipend placements, how will you support yourself for the full duration?

A common weak point across all J1 categories is being able to name a funding source but not explain whether it's actually sufficient for the full length of the program — be specific about amounts and timing, not just the source.

## Plans during and after the program

Practice questions:

- What do you plan to do once your program ends?
- Do you intend to return to your home country afterward?
- If you're a student or scholar, how does this exchange connect to your studies or career at home?
- Are you subject to the two-year home residency requirement, and if so, how does that fit your plans?

## Your DS-2019 and SEVIS record

Every J1 participant is issued a Form DS-2019 by their program sponsor, which is what makes the program official and lets you pay the SEVIS I-901 fee and schedule your visa interview. Your DS-2019 lists your specific program category, your sponsor, your program dates, and your funding — and, importantly, it shows whether the two-year home residency requirement (marked with a "(e)" designation in some cases) applies to your category.

Before your interview, read your own DS-2019 carefully rather than treating it as paperwork to hand over. Officers may ask about details that are printed right on it — your program end date, your sponsor's name, your category — and being unable to answer confidently about your own form is a preventable weak point.

## Home country ties

Practice questions:

- What ties do you have to your home country — family, work, or study commitments?
- Why are you doing this exchange instead of a similar opportunity at home?
- Do you have a job, place at university, or family responsibilities waiting for you?

As with other nonimmigrant categories, the strength of your ties to home is assessed alongside your program details — vague answers here can raise doubts even when a program itself is completely legitimate.

## Sample J1 interview questions to practice with

1. What is your J1 program category, and what will you be doing?
2. Who is your program sponsor, and how were you selected?
3. How is your participation funded, for the full length of the program?
4. What are your plans once the program ends?
5. What ties do you have to your home country?
6. Are you aware of whether the two-year home residency requirement applies to your category?
7. Have you traveled internationally before, or held a U.S. visa?

## Mistakes that weaken J1 interview answers

- **Vague program descriptions.** Being unable to clearly explain what your program actually involves — beyond "cultural exchange" — can raise doubts about its legitimacy, even when it's genuine.
- **Unclear funding.** If your funding source or amount doesn't clearly cover your program costs for its full duration, address the gap directly rather than hoping it isn't asked.
- **Thin home-country ties.** As with other nonimmigrant categories, weak or vague answers about your plans to return can be a sticking point.
- **Confusing your category's specifics.** An au pair program and a research scholar program involve very different day-to-day realities, funding structures, and sponsor relationships — be precise about your own, not a generic idea of "J1."
- **Not knowing about the two-year home residency requirement.** If it applies to your category, being caught off guard by the question looks worse than a straightforward, informed answer.
